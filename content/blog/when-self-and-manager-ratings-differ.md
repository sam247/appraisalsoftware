Most appraisal cycles produce at least one awkward moment: the employee rates themselves highly, the manager is more cautious, or the reverse. That gap is not a failure of the process. It is often the most useful signal the review produces.

This guide is for managers and small-team organisers who need a calm way to discuss differences without turning the meeting into a defence of scores.

## Why ratings diverge

Self and manager views often differ for ordinary reasons:

- The employee sees day-to-day effort; the manager sees outcomes and priorities across the team
- Expectations were never written down clearly enough
- Recent work overshadows the rest of the review period
- Feedback earlier in the year was sparse, so the appraisal becomes the first surprise

> Note: Treat a rating gap as a prompt for conversation, not as proof that one person is “wrong”.

## Prepare before the meeting

Do this work before you sit down together:

1. Re-read the role expectations and any objectives agreed for the period
2. Collect two or three concrete examples for each area where you differ
3. Decide what a good outcome looks like — clarity, not identical scores
4. Leave space for the employee’s evidence; do not open by announcing the “correct” rating

If you use Appraisal Software, review the paired self and manager responses side by side before the conversation so you are reacting to what was written, not to memory.

## Structure the conversation

Keep the meeting focused on evidence and expectations:

| Step | Focus | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open | Shared purpose of the review | Leading with the score gap |
| Explore | Specific examples from both sides | Vague labels like “attitude” |
| Align | What “good” means for the role | Relitigating personality |
| Close | One or two clear next steps | An overloaded action list |

A simple opener works well: “I noticed we saw this area differently. Can we walk through a couple of examples from each of us?”

## What to write down afterwards

After the meeting, record:

- Where you reached a shared view
- Where you still differ, honestly
- The support, priorities or objectives that follow

Do not force a single rating if the evidence genuinely supports two perspectives. An unresolved difference, noted clearly, is more useful than a false consensus.
