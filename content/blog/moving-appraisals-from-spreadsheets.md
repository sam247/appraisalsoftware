A spreadsheet can work for a small review exercise. Problems often arise when several managers use different versions, invitations become a manual chase, or responses are stored in inconsistent places. Moving to software should address those specific problems.

Do not begin by copying every historic file into a new system. First decide what belongs in the next review cycle and what remains in your existing records process.

## Map the current handoffs

Write down how a review moves from preparation to completion. Who chooses the questions? Who confirms manager assignments? Who sends the invitation? Who can read the finished record?

Mark the points where you currently lose time or certainty. This gives you a testable reason for changing tools. A new interface alone does not fix unclear responsibility.

## Prepare only the data you need

| Item | Check before setup |
| --- | --- |
| People | Current work email, correct name and role |
| Managers | The intended reviewer, not an old reporting line |
| Questions | Relevant prompts, required fields and a clear scale |
| Dates | Review period, send date, closing date and timezone |
| Access | Who organises the workspace and reads results |
| Historic files | Retention location and access under your existing policy |

Remove duplicate records and resolve uncertain assignments before sending. Do not upload sensitive historical comments merely because an import mechanism exists. The software's People import is not a historical-answer migration service.

## Pilot a complete workflow

For an employee appraisal pilot, prepare a short form and a small set of identified employee/manager assignments. Test the invitation, response and [Self vs Manager workflow](/employee-appraisal-software).

For an anonymous 360 pilot, use a separate campaign with a relevant reviewer cohort. Check the [360 privacy and report rules](/360-appraisals#privacy), including campaign closure and response thresholds. A spreadsheet that exposes individual reviewer comments is not equivalent to the product's combined reporting model.

Do not mix the two pilots into one assessment without explaining their different purposes.

## A fictional transition example

A 40-person organisation finds that its managers keep emailing revised annual forms to the organiser. It decides to test one consistent questionnaire with a small group before moving the next full cycle.

The organiser checks employee and manager email addresses, agrees the questions, and keeps historical reviews in the existing restricted records location. The pilot tests invitation delivery, response collection and completion visibility. Managers still agree development actions in their conversations.

The example illustrates a plan, not a customer case study. It does not imply that software automatically improves completion or quality.

## Decide when the old tracker stops

Choose which system is authoritative for the new cycle. Keeping two live lists indefinitely can create conflicting completion states. If you need a temporary checklist during the pilot, name its owner and decide when it is retired.

Retain records according to your organisation's policy. This article does not prescribe a retention period or replace professional advice about sensitive employment records.

## Check readiness before the wider rollout

- The pilot answers the original operational problem.
- Managers know where to prepare and view responses.
- Participants know what is shared and with whom.
- The organiser knows how reminders and deadlines behave.
- Capacity and evaluation terms fit the planned rollout.
- Historical records have a clear, appropriate home.
